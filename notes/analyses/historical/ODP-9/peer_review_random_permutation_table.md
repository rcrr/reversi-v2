## 1. Answers to the §9 v2 questions (the second review's mandate)

### Q1: Theorem 1 v2 — the ORACLE statement

> *"Is the ORACLE statement (given (position, counter); seed-averaged; A1) the right semantic level for the design?"*

**Yes.** The oracle statement is the correct level. Given a FIXED pair (p, c) — both inputs, nothing generated by the table — the G₁ pick is `P_k[c mod k]` where c mod k is a fixed residue and P_k (A1) is a uniform random permutation. Lemma 1 applies directly. No conditioning. No prefix. This is incontestable.

The oracle statement captures exactly what the implementation does at each call to `pick_move`: it takes the current legal bitmask and the current counter, and returns a move via `table[k][counter % k]`. The semantics of that operation, averaged over the seed, is "uniform pick from the legal set." That is the correct semantic level.

> *"Is the circularity analysis correct?"*

**Yes.** The author's entry-consumption analysis (§6.3: "uniform over the unconsumed entries, deterministic on reuse") is precisely correct. Here is the independent re-derivation:

Consider a generated trajectory. At a width-k ply, the counter has value c. Let m = c mod k. Let R be the set of m' ∈ {0..k-1} already consumed by EARLIER plies at the SAME width k (i.e., earlier counter values c' with c' mod k = m' and the same width k). The entry P_k[m] is a value v ∈ {0..k-1}. If m ∈ R, then v was already used by an earlier ply — the position at that earlier ply had the SAME rank value v, and the current pick reuses it. If m ∉ R, then v is uniformly distributed over the (k − |R|) values NOT in R — because the consumed entries took |R| distinct values, and conditional on those, the remaining entries of a uniform permutation are a uniform permutation of the remaining values.

The v1 proof claimed "P_k[c mod k] is uniform" without accounting for R. That was the circularity — conditioning on the prefix fixes R, and the conditional distribution is NOT uniform unless R = ∅. The author's decomposition is correct.

> *"Is the author's rejection of the first review's unconditional-marginal replacement correct?"*

**Yes. I was wrong.** Let me explain why.

My first review proposed: "Under A1, at ANY ply, the MARGINAL distribution of the G₁ pick, averaged over the seed WITHOUT conditioning on the trajectory prefix, is uniform over L."

This statement has two problems:

1. **L_t is a random variable at a fixed ply index t.** Different seeds produce different trajectories with DIFFERENT legal sets at the same ply index. "The marginal distribution of the pick is uniform over L" when L itself is random is not well-defined — you'd need to say "uniform over L for each fixed seed, then average over L," which is true for G₀ (the conditional uniformity + the Markov property) but for G₁ requires establishing that the seed-averaged distribution of (L_t, pick_t) factorizes correctly. The oracle statement avoids this by making L an INPUT.

2. **The counterexample is correct and fatal to my proposal.** The author's k = 2 counterexample (§6.3): define a table-dependent index I(P₂) = 1 if P₂ = [1,0], else 0. Then P₂[I(P₂)] is P₂[0] when P₂ ≠ [1,0] (which is 0 half the time), and P₂[1] when P₂ = [1,0] (which is 1). This is identically 0 — a point mass, not a uniform. **The marginal uniformity of P₂ does not imply the uniformity of P₂[I] for a table-dependent I.**

In G₁, c is table-dependent through the trajectory (a function of the entire table P, not just P_k). So my claim "the marginal of P_k[c mod k] is uniform because P_k is uniform" does not follow. The real defense is the gate.

**I retract my proposed Theorem 1 replacement.** The oracle statement is the valid one. My first review's §2.1 "Fix" should have been the oracle statement, not the unconditional-marginal one. I correct this below (§4).

### Q2: The reclassification (§6.4, §6.6)

> *"Is it correct that the corpus-level expectation equality (marginals AND joints) is NOT proven exactly in the generated regime and must be gated?"*

**Yes.** The v2 reclassification is correct and complete. The v1 claimed:

- E_seed[F^{G₁}] = E₀[F] exactly, for marginals (proven) and joints (proven).
- Both relied on Theorem 1's conditional-kernel claim + an exchange-of-averages argument.

The exchange-of-averages argument is: E_seed F^{G₁} = (1/|corpus|) Σ_{(g,t)} E_seed[I(g,t)]. Under v1's invalid Theorem 1, E_seed[I(g,t) | prefix] = P_G₀(I | prefix), so the summands are identical and the equality follows.

**But Theorem 1 does not provide E_seed[I(g,t) | prefix] = P_G₀(I | prefix).** The oracle statement gives the pick law at a GIVEN (p, c), not the pick law at a GENERATED (p, c). The generated (p, c) at ply t depends on the table's earlier picks — the prefix conditions the table, and the oracle statement is about an unconditioned table. The two are different: the oracle says "if you give me (p, c) from outside, I give you a uniform pick" — but the generator never receives (p, c) from outside; it generates them itself, using the same table.

So E_seed[I(g,t)] is NOT provably equal to the G₀ analog by this route. The v2 reclassification is honest and correct.

> *"Is the plausibility argument sound?"*

**Yes, with the caveat that it is a plausibility argument, not a proof.** The claim is: the local semantics are identical (oracle Theorem 1), so the AVERAGED path through the position graph follows the same transition weights as G₀ in expectation — this is the intuition that a Latin-hypercube-stratified quadrature of a Markov chain has the same expectation as the chain. This intuition is correct for standard Latin hypercube sampling of independent variables, but G₁ is not a standard LH design (the counter couples across widths). The large-N concentration argument is valid: per-seed corpus statistics concentrate around the seed average, and with N = 1e6 the seed average is well-approximated. But the systematic deviation (the entry-consumption effect at corpus scale) could create a BOUNDED bias that does not vanish with N — the gate catches it.

The plausibility argument is not a proof. The author does not claim it is a proof. This is the right balance.

> *"Does any argument RE-PROVE it?"*

**No.** I looked for one and didn't find it. The cleanest route would be to show that the G₁ corpus is a scrambled-net quadrature of the G₀ Markov chain (Owen 1997), which would give an explicit O(1/N) variance reduction. But G₁'s counter walk is not a standard (t,m,s)-net — the counter's deterministic walk across widths creates a specific coupling structure that resists net-theoretic analysis. I judge this as: **probably true, plausibly provable with non-trivial effort, and not needed because the gate is cheaper.**

### Q3: The design requirement (§5)

> *"Do the I-017 statistics tolerate the path-coupling deviation at the level the gate bounds?"*

**Yes.** The SU (symmetrical uncertainty) is computed from frequency tables via H = −Σ p log₂ p and MI = Σ p_xy log₂(p_xy/(p_x p_y)). These are continuous functions of the probability estimates. A perturbation of O(1/√N) in the p estimates propagates to O(1/√N) in H and I (by the delta method, since H and I are smooth away from p = 0). The G₀-vs-G₀ noise at 1e4 is ~1e-2 per cell; at 1e7 it is ~1e-3. The gate ensures the G₁ systematic deviation is **within** that G₀ noise envelope. So the SU values computed from G₁ at 1e7 differ from the "true" G₀ SU at 1e7 by at most ~1e-3 in the p estimates, which translates to ~1e-3 in H and MI — well below any practical significance threshold for feature selection/analysis.

Similarly, eta²/omega² from the DB set are independent of the corpus (they use the DB game values). The adherence metrics (chi-square, KL, TV) compare the DB distribution against the corpus IDEAL distribution. A 1e-3 perturbation in the ideal frequencies propagates to a ~1e-3 perturbation in TV and a ~O(1/N) perturbation in KL (the KL is sensitive near p = 0, but the merged-tail technique handles that). The gate threshold (G₀ noise) is the sufficient yardstick.

> *"Is the gate's threshold design the sufficient yardstick?"*

**Yes.** The criterion "G₁ must be no more different from G₀ than G₀ is from another G₀ run" is the right calibration. It means: the systematic G₁-vs-G₀ deviation is no larger than the irreducible Monte Carlo noise of the corpus itself. Since any downstream statistic is dominated by the Monte Carlo noise (via the delta method), the G₁ systematic deviation is invisible to the I-017 analyses.

> *"Is the fallback mechanically correct?"*

**Yes.** The second independent counter with a co-prime offset: c' = g + offset, with offset and N co-prime. At a same-width decision (k = k'), the two picks draw from P_k[c mod k] and P_k[c' mod k]. Since c and c' are independent counter walks (different starting phases, same deterministic increment), the two residues c mod k and c' mod k are, in expectation over the counter walks, independent — they are two draws from a uniform permutation at two indices that are not linked by the shared counter. The pair law is then P(P_k[a] = r, P_k[b] = r') for a ≠ b, which is 1/(k(k-1)) for r ≠ r' and 0 for r = r'. This restores the distinctness deviation (TV = 1/k at the rank level) — wait, actually it does NOT restore the G₀ pair law (which allows equality with probability 1/k). It gives UNIFORM DISTINCT PAIRS, same as the single-counter case. The difference is in the COUNTER coupling: with one counter, the two same-width picks are consecutive visits to the same permutation row with a shared counter, so the earlier consumption of entry P_k[m] affects the later pick. With two independent counters, the two walks visit the row at independent points — they CAN still produce the same rank if the permutation maps a ≠ b to the same value (which it does not — it's a permutation, so P_k[a] ≠ P_k[b] for a ≠ b). So the fallback also gives distinct pairs. 

**This means the fallback does NOT restore the G₀ pair law — it only removes the counter-coupling.** The residual distinctness constraint (TV = 1/k) remains in both G₁ and the fallback; the fallback removes only the counter-mediated coupling between the two same-width picks. The gate must measure whether the distinctness constraint at the rank level, propagated to the observable code level, is within G₀ noise. If it is not, the fallback won't help — and a THIRD counter is not a solution. The document should note this: the fallback removes the counter-coupling, not the permutation-distinctness constraint.

**I recommend adding a note in §7.8:** "The fallback removes the counter-mediated coupling but NOT the permutation-distinctness constraint (P_k[a] ≠ P_k[b] for a ≠ b). If the gate fails DUE TO the distinctness constraint (the same-width tests 4-5 fail but the cross-width tests pass), the fallback will also fail — a third counter would not help. In that case, the design reopens."

## 3. New issues identified in this second pass

### 3.1 The fallback does not restore the G₀ pair law

As noted in Q5 above: the second independent counter removes the counter-mediated coupling between same-width picks, but it does NOT remove the permutation-distinctness constraint (P_k[a] ≠ P_k[b] for a ≠ b). The fallback still produces DISTINCT pairs at the rank level, whereas G₀ allows equality with probability 1/k. The TV = 1/k at the rank level persists.

**Recommendation:** Add a note in §7.8 clarifying what the fallback DOES and DOES NOT fix.

### 3.2 The gate's sparse-cell challenge at 1e4 deserves a caveat

Test 3 (the joint chi-square on 1296 × 1296) notes that df ≈ 1.7e6, and "the chi-square is in the asymptotic regime." At 1e4 samples over ~1.7e6 cells, the average cell frequency is ~0.006 — severely sparse. The asymptotic chi-square approximation requires expected frequencies ≥ 1 (or ≥ 5 for the standard rule). The merged-tail technique merges cells with expected frequency < 5 into a single bin, but with average expected frequency ~0.006, the merge will combine MOST cells into the tail bin. The resulting chi-square has very few degrees of freedom (the number of unmerged cells, likely dozens, not millions). This test loses sensitivity.

**This is not a design problem** — it just means test 3 is less informative than tests 4-5 (which focus on the risky case and use TV/KL, which do not require the sparsity correction). I recommend adding a sentence in §7 noting that test 3 may have limited power at 1e4 and tests 4-5 are the primary joint-gate instruments.

### 3.3 The plausibility argument's Markov-chain intuition can be made more precise

The plausibility argument (§6.4) says: "the local semantics are identical (oracle Theorem 1), so the AVERAGED path through the position graph follows the same transition weights as G₀ in expectation."

This is correct if we treat the G₁ process as: at each step, the POSITION evolves according to the G₁ deterministic pick, but the oracle says that FROM ANY GIVEN (position, counter), the seed-averaged NEXT POSITION distribution is exactly the G₀ next-position distribution (uniform over the legal moves). If we consider the seed-averaged process as a Markov chain on the STATE (position, counter), the transition probabilities are:

P_seed( (p', c+1) | (p, c) ) = (1/k(p)) for each legal move from p → p'
P_seed( (p', c) | (p, c) ) = 1 for the pass from p → p' (no counter increment)

where k(p) = |L(p)|. This IS exactly the G₀ transition probabilities on positions, augmented with a counter that tracks the number of real moves.

**Now the path-coupling effect enters because the seed average is over the table, and the TABLE is FIXED per seed.** The per-seed process is NOT Markov with these transition probabilities — it is a deterministic walk through the position graph. The seed-averaged process IS Markov with these transition probabilities (by the oracle Theorem 1, which gives the 1-step transition). The MULTI-STEP seed-averaged distribution is the product of these 1-step transitions — exactly the G₀ multi-step distribution. So the seed-averaged marginal distribution of the position at ply t IS the G₀ distribution.

**But the corpus frequency is NOT E_seed[position at ply t = some position] — it is the empirical frequency of the generated positions, averaged over plies and games.** The seed average of this empirical frequency is NOT the same as the seed-averaged per-ply distribution, because the empirical frequency is a QUADRATIC form (a product of two indicators, through the batch average). The equality E_seed[(1/N) Σ f(x)] = (1/N) Σ E_seed[f(x)] holds by linearity, but the seed-averaged per-ply expectation E_seed[f(x)] is NOT necessarily the G₀ expectation of f(x) — we just argued it IS, for position-level observables, because the oracle gives the 1-step transition.

**Wait — does it?** The oracle Theorem 1 says: for a FIXED (p, c), E_seed[G₁ pick | (p, c)] = uniform over L(p). This means the seed-averaged 1-step position transition from a FIXED (p, c) is the G₀ transition. The seed-averaged position distribution at ply t evolves by the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation: the distribution at t+1 is the distribution at t convolved with the 1-step transition. This product is exactly the G₀ evolution.

**But this argument assumes that the distribution at ply t is INDEPENDENT of the table's behavior at step t.** The oracle Theorem 1 gives the conditional expectation for a FIXED (p, c). When we average over the seed, we integrate over the joint distribution of (table, position at ply t, counter at ply t). The position and counter at ply t are functions of the table (through the earlier picks). So E_seed[f(position at t+1)] = E_seed[ E_seed[f(position at t+1) | position at t, counter at t] ]. The inner expectation is over the seed, GIVEN (position at t, counter at t). **But "given (position at t, counter at t)" MEANS conditioning on the table, because (position at t, counter at t) are functions of the table.** This is the same circularity.

So the Markov-chain intuition does NOT close the gap either. The plausibility argument is still just a plausibility argument. The gate remains the defense.

**Recommendation:** Remove or qualify the phrase "so the AVERAGED path through the position graph follows the same transition weights as G₀ in expectation" — it suggests a proof that does not yet exist. Replace with: "the oracle gives the 1-step uniformity; the multi-step behavior is the gated question."
